# Day 6 – Proteins: DNA → RNA → Protein
Date: 24/10/26

## 1. Why proteins matter
- Genes are instructions. Proteins are the molecules that do the work in the cell.
- Examples of what proteins do:
  - Enzymes speed up reactions (e.g., amylase digests starch)
  - Structural support (collagen, keratin)
  - Transport (hemoglobin carries oxygen)
  - Defence (antibodies)
  - Signalling (insulin, receptors)

## 2. Building blocks: amino acids
- A protein is a chain of amino acids joined by peptide bonds.
- There are 20 standard amino acids.
- Every amino acid has the same backbone: an amino group (-NH2), a carboxyl group (-COOH), and a central carbon.
- The R group (side chain) is different for each amino acid, and it decides the amino acid's properties:
  - Hydrophobic (water-fearing)
  - Hydrophilic / polar
  - Charged (positive or negative)
- A chain of amino acids is called a polypeptide.

## 3. From gene to protein (recap + detail)
1. Transcription: DNA → mRNA (in the nucleus in eukaryotes)
2. mRNA leaves the nucleus
3. Translation: the ribosome reads mRNA in codons (3 bases = 1 amino acid)
4. tRNA brings the matching amino acid (its anticodon pairs with the codon)
5. Amino acids are linked into a chain until a stop codon is reached

Key codons:
- Start: AUG (codes for methionine)
- Stop: UAA, UAG, UGA (no amino acid)
- 64 codons, 20 amino acids → the genetic code is redundant (several codons can code for the same amino acid)

## 4. Four levels of protein structure
| Level | What it is |
|---|---|
| Primary | The sequence of amino acids |
| Secondary | Local folding: alpha helix and beta sheet (held by hydrogen bonds) |
| Tertiary | The full 3D shape of one chain (R-group interactions, disulfide bonds) |
| Quaternary | Several chains assembled together (e.g., hemoglobin has 4) |

## 5. Structure = function
- The shape of a protein decides what it can do.
- Enzymes have an active site that fits their substrate, like a lock and key.
- Wrong shape = protein may not work.
- Denaturation: heat or pH changes unfold a protein and it loses its function (e.g., egg white turning solid when cooked).

## 6. Mutations and proteins
- A mutation changes the DNA, which can change the mRNA, which can change the amino acid sequence.
- Types by effect on the protein:
  - Silent: same amino acid (because of redundancy), no change
  - Missense: a different amino acid
  - Nonsense: a stop codon appears early, giving a short, usually useless protein
  - Frameshift: an insertion or deletion shifts all the codons after it
- Example: sickle cell disease. One base change in the hemoglobin gene swaps one amino acid (glutamic acid → valine). Hemoglobin then clumps and red blood cells become sickle-shaped.

## 7. Link to disease and drug discovery
- Many diseases come from faulty or missing proteins.
- Most drugs work by binding to a protein (an enzyme or receptor) and blocking or activating it.
- To design a drug, scientists need to know the 3D structure of the target protein.
- Example: many antibiotics target bacterial proteins, such as ribosomes or cell wall enzymes.

## 8. Link to bioinformatics
- Protein sequences are stored in databases such as UniProt.
- 3D structures are stored in the Protein Data Bank (PDB).
- AlphaFold predicts protein 3D structure from the amino acid sequence.

## Key terms
amino acid, peptide bond, polypeptide, codon, anticodon, tRNA, ribosome, denaturation, active site, missense, nonsense, frameshift
