## 1. What are microbes?

- Microbes (microorganisms) are living things too small to see without a microscope.
- Main groups: **bacteria, archaea, fungi, protozoa**, plus **viruses** (not truly cells, see section 5).
- Most microbes are harmless or helpful. Gut bacteria help digestion, and bacteria are used to make medicines such as insulin.
- A small number are **pathogens**: microbes that cause disease.
- The community of microbes living in and on the body is the **microbiome**.

---

## 2. Bacteria: structure

- Bacteria are **prokaryotes**: single-celled, with **no nucleus** and no membrane-bound organelles.
- Typical size: about 1-5 µm.
- Main parts:
  - **Nucleoid:** region holding the single, usually circular chromosome
  - **Plasmids:** small extra circles of DNA, often carrying useful genes (e.g., antibiotic resistance)
  - **Ribosomes (70S):** make proteins. They are different from human ribosomes (80S), which matters for drugs.
  - **Cell wall** made of **peptidoglycan**: gives shape and protection. Human cells have no peptidoglycan.
  - **Cell membrane** inside the wall
  - Optional extras: **capsule** (protective coat), **flagella** (movement), **pili** (attachment and DNA transfer)
- Common shapes:
  - **Cocci** (spheres)
  - **Bacilli** (rods)
  - **Spirilla** (spirals)

### Gram staining
- A lab test that sorts bacteria by cell wall type.
- **Gram-positive:** thick peptidoglycan layer, stain purple.
- **Gram-negative:** thin peptidoglycan layer plus an outer membrane, stain pink.
- The cell wall type affects which antibiotics work.

### Bacterial genomes
- Small and compact: roughly 1-10 million base pairs.
- Example: *E. coli* has about 4.6 million base pairs and around 4,000+ genes.
- Little non-coding DNA, which makes bacterial genomes easier to analyze.

---

## 3. How bacteria reproduce and change

- **Binary fission:** one cell copies its DNA and splits into two identical cells.
- Can be very fast. *E. coli* can divide about every 20 minutes in ideal conditions.
- Fast division plus **mutations** (Day 4) means bacterial populations evolve quickly.
- Bacteria also share genes directly (**horizontal gene transfer**):
  - **Conjugation:** DNA passes through a pilus from one cell to another (often plasmids)
  - **Transformation:** a cell takes up free DNA from its surroundings
  - **Transduction:** a virus carries DNA from one bacterium to another

|---|---|

| Cholera | *Vibrio cholerae* |
| Typhoid | *Salmonella* Typhi |
| Strep throat | *Streptococcus pyogenes* |
| Urinary tract infections | often *E. coli* |

- Bacteria harm the body by:
  - releasing **toxins**
  - invading and damaging tissue
  - triggering a strong immune response

## 5. Viruses: structure

- Viruses are **not cells**. They have no cytoplasm, no ribosomes and no metabolism of their own.
- They are much smaller than bacteria (roughly 20-300 nm).
- Basic parts:
  - **Genome:** DNA or RNA (never both), single or double stranded
  - **Capsid:** protein coat around the genome
  - **Envelope:** an outer lipid layer in some viruses (e.g., influenza, HIV, SARS-CoV-2)
  - **Surface proteins:** used to attach to host cells
- Viruses are **obligate intracellular parasites**: they can only reproduce inside a host cell.
- Whether viruses count as "alive" is debated, since they only show life features inside a host.
- Viral genomes are tiny. HIV has about 9,000 bases and SARS-CoV-2 about 30,000.
- A virus that infects bacteria is called a **bacteriophage**.

---

## 6. How viruses replicate

1. **Attachment:** surface proteins bind a specific receptor on the host cell (this is why viruses infect only certain cells or species).
2. **Entry:** the virus or its genome gets inside the cell.
3. **Replication:** the virus uses the host's machinery to copy its genome and make viral proteins.
4. **Assembly:** new virus particles are put together.
5. **Release:** new viruses leave the cell, sometimes destroying it.

### Two cycles in bacteriophages
- **Lytic:** the phage replicates at once and bursts the cell open.
- **Lysogenic:** the phage DNA inserts into the host chromosome and is copied silently with it until it is triggered to become lytic.

### Retroviruses
- RNA viruses such as **HIV** carry **reverse transcriptase**, which copies RNA into DNA.
- This reverses the usual direction of the central dogma (RNA → DNA).
- The DNA copy is inserted into the host genome.

## 7. Viruses that cause disease

| Disease | Virus | Genome |
|---|---|---|

| COVID-19 | SARS-CoV-2 | RNA |

- RNA viruses often mutate fast because copying RNA is error-prone. This is why flu vaccines are updated regularly.

## 8. Bacteria vs viruses

| Feature | Bacteria | Viruses |
|---|---|---|
| Cells? | Yes, single-celled | No |
| Size | About 1-5 µm | About 20-300 nm |
| Genome | DNA (circular chromosome + plasmids) | DNA or RNA |
| Own ribosomes and metabolism | Yes | No |
| Reproduction | Binary fission, on their own | Only inside a host cell |
| Treated with | Antibiotics | Antivirals, vaccines |

---

## 9. Treatment and resistance

### Antibiotics (bacteria only)
- They target features bacteria have but human cells do not:
  - **Cell wall synthesis** (e.g., penicillin)
  - **70S ribosomes** (e.g., tetracyclines)
  - **DNA replication enzymes** (e.g., fluoroquinolones target DNA gyrase)
- Antibiotics do **not** work on viruses.

### Antivirals and vaccines
- Antivirals target viral proteins or steps in the virus cycle (e.g., HIV reverse transcriptase inhibitors, protease inhibitors).
- Vaccines train the immune system to recognise a pathogen before infection.

### Antibiotic resistance
- Random mutations or gained genes make some bacteria survive a drug.
- Antibiotic use kills sensitive bacteria and the resistant ones survive and multiply (**natural selection**).
- Resistance genes spread by horizontal gene transfer, often on plasmids.
- Resistance is a major global health problem and a big focus of drug discovery.

## 10. Bioinformatics connection (preview only)

- Microbial genomes are small, so they were among the first genomes sequenced and analyzed.
- Sequencing helps to:
  - identify an unknown microbe
  - track outbreaks by comparing genomes of different samples
  - find resistance genes
  - discover new drug targets
- Resources to meet later:
  - **NCBI:** genome and sequence databases
  - **CARD:** database of antibiotic resistance genes

## 11. Key terms

- **Pathogen:** microbe that causes disease.
- **Prokaryote:** cell with no nucleus.
- **Plasmid:** small circular DNA in bacteria.
- **Peptidoglycan:** bacterial cell wall material.
- **Gram staining:** test that sorts bacteria by cell wall type.
- **Binary fission:** bacterial cell division.
- **Horizontal gene transfer:** sharing genes between cells (conjugation, transformation, transduction).
- **Capsid:** protein coat of a virus.
- **Envelope:** outer lipid layer of some viruses.
- **Bacteriophage:** virus that infects bacteria.
- **Obligate intracellular parasite:** needs a host cell to reproduce.
- **Reverse transcriptase:** enzyme that makes DNA from RNA.
- **Antibiotic resistance:** bacteria survive drugs that once killed them.

---

## 12. Quick recap

- Bacteria are single cells with their own machinery. Viruses are not cells and need a host.
- Bacteria divide by binary fission and swap genes by conjugation, transformation and transduction.
- Viruses attach, enter, replicate, assemble and release.
- Antibiotics target bacterial structures, so they do not work on viruses.
- Mutations plus selection lead to resistance in bacteria and fast change in RNA viruses.
- Microbial genomes are small, which makes them a good starting point for bioinformatics.
